Nvidia Halts AI Chip Shipments to Southeast Asia, Boosting Korean Semiconductor Stocks

Nvidia export curbs spark surge in Korea’s semiconductor and data center sectors

💹 Finance 🤖 AI · Tech ⚡ Energy 📈 Stocks
Finance Financial Markets

The KOSPI rose 0.7% to close at 2,860 points while the KOSDAQ gained 0.5% finishing at 930 points. Foreign investors predominantly bought large-cap stocks, driven by strong U.S. tech performance and a weakening dollar. Semiconductor, secondary battery, and data center-related shares surged between 2% and 4%. Meanwhile, the U.S. 10-year Treasury yield modestly rebounded to 4.25%, raising some concerns about potential capital outflows from emerging markets.

💡 Analyst Insights
Southeast Asia’s AI chip export restrictions have directly benefited Korea's semiconductor, data center, and network equipment sectors. Samsung Electronics is positioned to strengthen its semiconductor supply chain and benefit from escalating DRAM demand, while SK Hynix, the market leader in high-bandwidth memory (HBM), is expected to see increased market share. In contrast, U.S. server supplier Super Micro Computer may experience short-term profit declines due to Nvidia’s disrupted Asian sales. Investors should consider increasing allocations to Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix over the next three months. However, easing of U.S. export controls or competitive responses from Taiwan’s TSMC could alter the outlook.
Tech AI · Tech Trends

Nvidia’s suspension of AI server chip shipments to Southeast Asia destabilizes the global AI hardware supply chain. Concurrently, the blockbuster launch of Grand Theft Auto VI, generating $1 billion in three days, underscores sustained investor appetite for human-driven gaming content amid AI advancements. Leading AI technology firms like OpenAI and Anthropic are integrating services, accelerating paid adoption. Korean tech giants Naver and Kakao have publicly committed to expanding investments in proprietary semiconductors and data center infrastructure to compete in AI leadership.

Nvidia halts AI chip shipments to Southeast Asia amid U.S. export tightening
Nvidia has instructed partners to pause server chip shipments to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Indonesia reflecting intensified U.S. efforts to restrict AI chip access to China. This move disrupts a critical segment of the global AI hardware supply chain. South Korean semiconductor companies, including Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, stand to gain from increased demand as buyers seek alternate suppliers. Investors should monitor U.S. regulatory developments closely as they will shape the supply landscape and competitive dynamics in Asian semiconductor markets.

💡 Analyst Insights
The AI chip supply pause positively impacts Korea’s semiconductor, system semiconductor, and data center industries. SK Hynix is expected to benefit from large-scale HBM shipments supporting AI server growth, while Samsung Electronics strengthens memory and foundry capabilities. Naver Cloud’s ongoing data center investments further capitalize on this trend. Globally, American memory firm Micron may leverage Nvidia’s supply constraints for market gains. We recommend increasing exposure to semiconductor and network equipment stocks in Korea over the next quarter. Nonetheless, risks remain from intensified U.S.-China export regulations and competitive pressures from Huawei, TSMC, and MediaTek’s alternative technologies.
Energy Energy · Industry

Australia’s new legislation mandates LNG exporters to reserve 20% of production for domestic consumption, intensifying price volatility in Asian natural gas markets. International crude prices remain elevated near $84 per barrel, supported by geopolitical tensions in Libya and other supply uncertainties. Policy shifts in the U.S. and Europe favor distributed renewable energy generation, impacting global energy supply strategies. These developments directly influence South Korea’s energy procurement and transition policy amid its status as a net importer of oil and gas.

Australia clears law requiring LNG exporters to keep 20% for domestic use
Australia's Parliament has passed a new law requiring liquefied natural gas (LNG) exporters to reserve 20% of their production for domestic use. The legislation aims to address local energy shortages and stabilize gas prices amid growing demand. This move may tighten export volumes, potentially impacting LNG supply and prices in international markets. Investors should watch how this policy affects Australia's LNG export revenues and global gas market dynamics going forward.

Rising Asian natural gas prices benefit Korean firms like Hanwha and Samsung Engineering, which are involved in LNG plant construction, as well as SK Gas that handles LNG import and sales. Conversely, large LNG consumers such as POSCO and Korean Air face increased fuel cost pressures, potentially dampening short-term stock performance. Chevron and other global LNG drilling and shipping companies stand to gain from elevated energy prices. Investors should consider short-term overweight positions in Hanwha and SK Gas. Key risks include escalating disputes with the Australian government, increased competition from alternative supply sources such as China, and demand contraction due to a global economic slowdown.
